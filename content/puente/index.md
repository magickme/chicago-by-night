## Who Is She

She was born in a village in Chalatenango province that no longer exists. The war came in 1980 and she chose a side -- not the guerrillas, not the government, but the machinery between them. The Atlacatl Battalion needed people who understood rural communities from the inside: who talked to whom, where the sympathizers hid food, which families would break and which had to be removed whole. Puente understood. She had run a boarding house for displaced campesinos in San Salvador and she knew how dependency worked -- how a woman who controls the roof and the meals and the medicine controls everything underneath them. The Battalion gave her a territory. She gave them clean villages. Not empty -- clean. The kind of clean where the next unit through finds laundry on the lines and beans on the stove and no one.

She was at El Mozote in December 1981, not pulling triggers but walking the perimeter afterward, identifying which houses to burn and which to leave standing so the helicopters would photograph a hamlet, not a massacre. She was already old then. She was already burying people in gardens then. The death squads loved her because she made their work look like weather -- like people had simply blown away.

## Before

Lucia Elena Puente was born in 1937 in a village outside Chalatenango, El Salvador. Her father grew corn and beans. Her mother kept a boarding house for seasonal laborers -- four rooms, shared kitchen, the smell of pupusas and wood smoke and lye soap. It was the center of the village's social life. Lucia's mother knew everyone's business because everyone ate at her table.

The village was burned in 1952 during La Matanza's long aftershocks -- a landlord dispute that became a military exercise. Lucia was fifteen. She walked to San Salvador with her mother's rosary and a photograph she kept in her wallet and did not look at. She opened her own boarding house by twenty. She understood the mechanics: dependency creates loyalty, loyalty creates information, information creates power. The women who cooked for soldiers heard things. The old men who swept courtyards saw things. The boardinghouse keeper who controlled the roof controlled it all.

By the time the civil war formalized in 1980, she was forty-three and had been running intelligence for the security forces for a decade -- not from ideology, but from the same instinct that made her mother the center of village life. She knew how communities worked because she had grown up inside one, and she knew how to take them apart because hers had been taken apart in front of her. The Atlacatl Battalion recognized her value. She recognized theirs: impunity, resources, and a government that did not ask questions about villages that went quiet.

## The Embrace

Dirlewanger found her in 1983. He had been rat-lined through the Vatican corridors in 1945, the French custody death a convenience, and had spent four decades selling the only skill set he respected to anyone who could afford it -- Stroessner's Paraguay, Pinochet's Chile, the Argentine junta's Process of National Reorganization. By the eighties he was in Central America because that was where the Americans were spending money, and the Americans always needed someone to train the men who trained the men who entered the villages at dawn.

He recognized Puente immediately. Not her methods, which were peasant-crude. Her *disposition*. The grandmother who understands that a community is an organism and that organisms can be disassembled by someone who knows where the joints are. He Embraced her in a safe house outside San Salvador that smelled like bleach and copal incense, and he taught her Vicissitude the way he taught everything -- through application on live subjects. She learned fast. She was better at it than he was within a decade, because Dirlewanger needed the suffering to be visible and Puente needed it to be *domestic*.

He is still in Chile. She does not visit.

## The Unlife

Vicissitude changed everything. The first time she reshaped her own hand -- extending the fingers by half an inch, feeling the bone flow like warm wax beneath the skin -- the old stillness broke. Not into chaos. Into purpose. The flesh was not fixed. The flesh was a medium that could be written. She adopted the Path of Power and the Inner Voice within six months -- not Metamorphosis, because Puente does not seek transcendence. She seeks sovereignty.

She arrived in Montreal in 1988 through The Rose's network -- one Tzimisce vouching for another. She claimed Chinatown because nobody else wanted it. She learned Cantonese from the merchants. She made herself useful to the community's power structure without revealing what she was. Vintage housecoat. Readers on a beaded chain. Grey hair she has never once restyled. The body underneath the grandmother costume has been Vicissitude'd so many times that what walks through her boarding house is a shell around a shell around something that no longer maps to human skeletal architecture, but the face -- the face she keeps. Soft. Lined. Trustworthy.

She has a building now. Six units. The tenants smile and carry groceries up the stairs and their movements are almost right. She is working toward something larger -- a full town, somewhere warm, where every face is sculpted and every routine is choreographed and the church bells ring on Sunday and the children play in the square and none of it is real and all of it is hers. She has been building home her entire life, because the original was burned before she could memorize it fully, and she will get it right this time even if she has to make every person in it from scratch. The boarding house is the prototype. Chalatenango is the blueprint. The difference is that this time, when she cleans a village, she fills it back up again.

When Calvi assembled the Fossoyeurs, Puente accepted on a condition: Chinatown was hers. Calvi agreed. The tension between ductus authority and domain sovereignty became the pack's productive friction. She is also the pack's heaviest weapon -- forty-seven years of Dirlewanger's institutional memory filtered through Vicissitude and applied with the quiet efficiency of a woman who has been burying bodies in gardens since before the Embrace.

## What She Wants

**Home.** The full town. The perfect reconstruction. Every face sculpted, every routine choreographed, every Sunday bell rung on schedule. She does not call it art. She calls it correction.

**Sovereignty that owes nothing to any hierarchy.** She holds Chinatown as a voivode holds domain -- absolutely, personally. The pack operates within it by her permission. This distinction matters.

## What She Fears

**Stasis.** The arrested state. The failure to build. A Tzimisce who stops transforming has accepted the draft as the final text.

**Dirlewanger arriving.** Not because she cannot destroy him. Because his presence would remind her that what she does -- the clean villages, the sculpted tenants, the grandmother's smile -- is a refinement of what he taught her, and refinement is not the same as escape.
